\begin{abstract}
Structured decisions---selecting among options, verifying whether evidence
supports a claim, and grading response quality on ordinal scales---are the
atomic operations of automated scientific pipelines. Proprietary
``System-One'' models deliver such decisions as typed API calls without
generation, but are closed. We present an open, trainable counterpart: a
frozen masked-diffusion language model backbone (LLaDA-8B) with small
specialist decision heads producing probabilities for three decision types
(choice, noul, score). We contribute three findings. (1)~A deterministic
canonical ordering of options yields exact permutation invariance
(option-order flip rate $0.77 \to 0.00$), where train-time logit averaging
does not. (2)~Multi-layer attention pooling over option spans outperforms
last-layer mean pooling for choice ($+7$ points), while an ordinal auxiliary
loss improves graded scoring ($+9$ points). (3)~Scaling training data
$30\times$ on an out-of-domain corpus improves in-distribution accuracy to
$0.96$ but does not transfer ($-13$ points on the target domain), showing
that coverage of the target reasoning distribution---not volume---is the
binding constraint. On public benchmarks, \ours{} reaches 0.853 on
SciFact claim verification and transfers zero-shot to general
classification (0.930 SST-2, 0.854 AG News), while parametric
knowledge tasks remain bounded by the backbone ceiling (0.315 GPQA
vs.\ backbone $\approx$0.33).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Automated scientific pipelines---agentic literature review, claim
verification, data extraction, routing to slower reasoning
components---are built out of small structured judgments: \emph{which}
of these options answers the question, \emph{does} the evidence support
the claim, \emph{how} good is this candidate answer. Today these
judgments are typically extracted from generative LLMs by prompting for
JSON and parsing the output, a pipeline that is slow, brittle under
format drift, and poorly calibrated.

A recent class of models removes generation entirely: given an
unstructured state and a set of typed questions, a \emph{System-One}
model returns typed values---a choice among options, a yes/no
probability, an ordinal score---each with a calibrated probability, in a
single forward pass. The flagship proprietary model of this class is
Jev (TypeSafe AI, 2026); open reimplementations appeared within days of
its release (kev, openjev, laya, SemIf), all built on autoregressive or
encoder backbones.

We ask what an open System-One model needs to be good at, and build one
on the other major family of language models: a \emph{masked-diffusion}
backbone (LLaDA-8B), whose bidirectional attention is a natural fit for
decision readouts that compare each option against the full context.
Rather than fine-tuning the backbone---which we show causes catastrophic
forgetting---we train small specialist heads per decision type on frozen
multi-layer hidden states. Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Exact permutation invariance by canonical ordering.}
Sorting options deterministically by content makes the presentation
order a pure function of the option set: the option-order flip rate is
$0.00$ by construction, where train-time logit averaging neither
achieves invariance nor preserves accuracy.
\item \textbf{Specialist heads per decision type.} Multi-layer
attention pooling over option spans improves choice ($+7$ points over
mean pooling) while hurting binary verification---different decision
types want different readouts. An ordinal auxiliary loss improves
graded scoring ($+9$ points).
\item \textbf{The backbone is the knowledge bound.} A $30\times$
out-of-domain data increase fails to transfer ($-13$ points), and on
GPQA the head reaches the backbone's reported ceiling ($0.315$ vs.\
$\approx0.33$): decision accuracy on knowledge tasks is a property of
the backbone, not the readout. Domain knowledge must be injected into
the weights---which we do via LoRA domain-adaptive pretraining on
scientific full texts.
\end{itemize}

We evaluate on a passage-grounded scientific decision battery and on
public benchmarks (GPQA, SciFact, and four general classification
sets), reporting accuracy, calibration, option-order flip rate, and
automation at 5\% error.

\section{Setup}
\paragraph{Decision types.}
We consider three typed decisions over a state (context) and a question:
\textbf{choice} selects one of $K$ answer options; \textbf{noul} decides
whether evidence supports a claim ($K{=}2$); \textbf{score} assigns an
ordinal grade $0 < 1 < 2$ (unrelated / related-but-wrong / correct).

\paragraph{Model.}
Each example is encoded as a single masked sequence: context, question, and
options separated by marker tokens. A frozen masked-diffusion backbone
(LLaDA-8B-Instruct) produces token representations; a specialist head per
decision type maps pooled option-span features to class logits. Two head
architectures are compared: a mean-pooled MLP and \emph{AttnPool}, a learned
query attending over each option span on concatenated hidden states from
layers $\{8,16,24,32\}$. Only the heads are trained ($\sim$2M parameters);
the backbone remains frozen.

\paragraph{Canonical ordering.}
Options are sorted by token-id content before encoding, and gold indices
remapped. The presentation order is thus a pure function of the option
\emph{set}: any input permutation collapses to the same sequence and
permutation invariance holds exactly, at training, calibration, and
evaluation.

\paragraph{Ordinal auxiliary loss.}
For score we add a CORAL-style term
$\sum_{j=1}^{K-1} \mathrm{BCE}\big(P(y \ge j),\,\mathbf{1}[g \ge j]\big)$
on cumulative softmax mass, respecting the grade order without changing the
$K$-way decode.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Accuracy, expected calibration error (ECE), option-order flip rate (accuracy
change under input permutation), and \emph{automation@5\%err}: the fraction
of decisions that can be auto-accepted while keeping realized error $\le
5\%$---the operating metric for a decision layer.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Scientific decision battery}
The \emph{elite} battery is built from passage-grounded scientific QA
typed by reasoning kind (factual, causal, multihop, negation,
numerical, definitional); each passage generates a choice item ($K{=}4$,
one gold + three distractors), a noul item (supported vs.\ unsupported
claim), and a score item (correct / related-but-wrong / unrelated
answer). Splits are passage-disjoint to prevent leakage
(2{,}240/6{,}720/6{,}720 train rows per type). The \emph{v2} battery
scales the same construction to 25{,}000 passages from three raw QA
corpora (physics/polymer LaTeX full texts, astronomy, and a derived
set) with groundedness filters---content-word recall $\ge 0.5$ and
numeric binding---yielding $\sim$470{,}000 training decisions; we use
it to probe the data-scaling question.

\subsection{Public benchmarks}
GPQA main (448 graduate-level questions) and diamond subset (198) as
choice; SciFact dev (340 claim--abstract pairs) as noul and 3-way score;
plus general classification (SST-2, AG News, Enron spam, Banking77) to
probe out-of-domain transfer. Benchmarks are eval-only; temperature is
fitted on the battery dev split (no benchmark leakage).

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What a decision layer buys.}
The value of a System-One component is not only accuracy but
\emph{operating characteristics}: determinism (canonical ordering makes
predictions a pure function of the option set), calibrated
probabilities (ECE $0.03$--$0.12$ after temperature fitting), and a
usable automation budget---82\% of choice traffic on our scientific
battery can be auto-accepted at $\le$5\% realized error, which is what
makes routing to a slower System-Two component a quantified decision
rather than a heuristic.

\paragraph{Division of labor between backbone and head.}
Our results consistently point to a clean separation: the readout
determines \emph{how well accessible information is used} (pooling,
ordering, ordinality), while the backbone determines \emph{what is
knowable at all} (GPQA at backbone ceiling; zero-shot transfer of
grounding skills to SST-2/AG News; collapse on $K{=}77$ for lack of
training coverage). This suggests the development loop for open
decision models: keep the head recipe fixed, improve the backbone's
domain knowledge (domain-adaptive pretraining, or a natively trained
domain backbone), and treat benchmarks like GPQA as backbone probes.

\paragraph{Relation to the ecosystem.}
Concurrent open reimplementations (kev, openjev, laya) share the API
contract but differ in backbone and readout; none is diffusion-based,
and none reports exact permutation invariance---our canonical ordering
achieves it by construction rather than by augmentation. Third-party
measurements of the proprietary reference on public datasets (Kumar,
2026) place it well above all open entries on general classification;
on scientific verification the margin is much smaller (SciFact noul:
ours $0.853$ vs.\ reported $\sim$0.89).

\section{Limitations}
Backbone pretraining may include benchmark-adjacent text (the standard
public-benchmark caveat). Heads are trained on one decision
distribution; $K>4$ degrades (option tournamenting is future work), and
cross-domain transfer is limited by construction. Score remains the
weakest decision type for all measured systems, including the
proprietary reference. Our proprietary-reference comparisons cite
third-party reports rather than our own measurements.
