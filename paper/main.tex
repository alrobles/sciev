\begin{abstract}
Structured decisions---selecting among options, verifying whether evidence
supports a claim, and grading response quality on ordinal scales---are the
atomic operations of automated scientific pipelines. Proprietary
``System-One'' models deliver such decisions as typed API calls without
generation, but are closed. We present an open, trainable counterpart: a
frozen masked-diffusion language model backbone (LLaDA-8B) with small
specialist decision heads producing probabilities for three decision types
(choice, noul, score). We contribute three findings. (1)~A deterministic
canonical ordering gives a permutation-invariant input sequence for
options with distinct token representations, rather than relying on
train-time order augmentation. (2)~Multi-layer attention pooling over
option spans outperforms last-layer mean pooling for choice ($+7$ points),
and the combined specialist, canonical, and ordinal recipe improves graded
scoring ($+9$ points).
(3)~Scaling training data $30\times$ on an out-of-domain corpus improves
in-distribution accuracy to $0.96$ but does not transfer ($-13$ points on
the target domain), suggesting that volume alone is insufficient. On
public benchmarks, the release heads reach 0.8500 on SciFact claim
verification under the legacy conversion but 0.464 under the canonical
\texttt{systemone-v2} encoding---an encoding sensitivity of the release
heads---while evidence-corruption retraining removes the option-side
shortcut and recovers 0.693. The heads transfer zero-shot to general classification
(0.9300 SST-2, 0.8537 AG News); knowledge-intensive GPQA remains
difficult (0.3125 on main).
Candidates using LoRA domain adaptation score higher on elite choice
but lower on GPQA and SciFact. Their head-training schedules also differ,
so this is a candidate comparison, not an isolated causal test of DAPT.
The release retains the frozen-backbone heads.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Automated scientific pipelines---agentic literature review, claim
verification, data extraction, routing to slower reasoning
components---are built out of small structured judgments: \emph{which}
of these options answers the question, \emph{does} the evidence support
the claim, \emph{how} good is this candidate answer. Today these
judgments are typically extracted from generative LLMs by prompting for
JSON and parsing the output, a pipeline that is slow, brittle under
format drift, and poorly calibrated.

A recent class of models removes generation entirely: given an
unstructured state and a set of typed questions, a \emph{System-One}
model returns typed values---a choice among options, a yes/no
probability, an ordinal score---each with a calibrated probability, in a
single forward pass. The flagship proprietary model of this class is
Jev (TypeSafe AI, 2026); open reimplementations appeared within days of
its release (kev, openjev, laya, SemIf), all built on autoregressive or
encoder backbones.

We ask what an open System-One model needs to be good at, and build one
on the other major family of language models: a \emph{masked-diffusion}
backbone (LLaDA-8B), whose bidirectional attention is a natural fit for
decision readouts that compare each option against the full context.
To preserve the pretrained weights and limit trainable parameters, we
train specialist heads per decision type on frozen multi-layer hidden
states. Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Permutation invariance by canonical ordering.}
Sorting options deterministically by content makes the presentation
order a function of the option set. For distinct token representations,
permutations produce the same input sequence; train-time order averaging
alone does not impose this constraint.
\item \textbf{Specialist heads per decision type.} Multi-layer
attention pooling over option spans improves choice ($+7$ points over
mean pooling) while hurting binary verification---different decision
types want different readouts. The combined specialist, canonical, and
ordinal recipe improves graded scoring ($+9$ points).
\item \textbf{Data volume and adaptation are not sufficient.} A
$30\times$ out-of-domain data increase fails to transfer ($-13$ points).
We also compare release candidates with and without LoRA domain
adaptation. The adapted candidates improve some elite metrics but lose
accuracy on GPQA and SciFact. We retain the frozen-backbone candidate;
matching the head optimization schedules is necessary before drawing
causal conclusions about adaptation.
\end{itemize}

We evaluate on the elite scientific battery, GPQA, SciFact, and four
classification datasets. Metrics cover accuracy, calibration, permutation
consistency, and retrospective automation at 5\% error.

\section{Setup}
\paragraph{Decision types.}
We consider three typed decisions over a state (context) and a question:
\textbf{choice} selects one of $K$ answer options; \textbf{noul} decides
whether evidence supports a claim ($K{=}2$); \textbf{score} assigns an
ordinal grade $0 < 1 < 2$ (unrelated / related-but-wrong / correct).

\paragraph{Model.}
The released span-pooling heads encode context, question, and options as
one observed token sequence, without inserting mask markers or generating
text. A frozen masked-diffusion backbone (LLaDA-8B-Instruct) produces token
representations. A mean-pooled MLP reads final-layer option spans for
noul and score; \emph{AttnPool} learns a query for each of layers
$\{8,16,24,32\}$ and concatenates the pooled features for choice. The
released choice head has 67{,}166{,}209 parameters; each MLP has
16{,}793{,}601. Only these heads are trained during decision training.

\paragraph{Canonical ordering.}
Options are sorted by token-id content before encoding, and gold indices
remapped. For options that remain distinct after tokenization and
truncation, any input permutation collapses to the same sequence.
Identical or truncated-to-identical options require a separate tie policy
if their external identifiers must remain distinguishable.

\paragraph{Ordinal auxiliary loss.}
For score we add a CORAL-style term
$\sum_{j=1}^{K-1} \mathrm{BCE}\big(P(y \ge j),\,\mathbf{1}[g \ge j]\big)$
on cumulative softmax mass, respecting the grade order without changing the
$K$-way decode.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We report accuracy, expected calibration error (ECE), option-order flip
rate (a change in the selected option's original identity after
permutation), and \emph{automation@5\%err}: the largest confidence-ranked
prefix with realized error $\le 5\%$. Automation is retrospective on
labeled evaluation data, not a guarantee of deployment error. Historical
non-canonical flip figures compared output positions without remapping
option identities; they are excluded here pending re-evaluation. The
release fixes that metric and includes regression tests.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Scientific decision battery}
The \emph{elite} battery is built from passage-grounded scientific QA
typed by reasoning kind (factual, causal, multihop, negation,
numerical, definitional); each passage generates a choice item ($K{=}4$,
one gold + three distractors), a noul item (supported vs.\ unsupported
claim), and a score item (correct / related-but-wrong / unrelated
answer). Splits are passage-disjoint to prevent leakage
(2{,}240/6{,}720/6{,}720 train rows per type). The \emph{v2} battery
scales the same construction to 25{,}000 passages from three raw QA
corpora (physics/polymer LaTeX full texts, astronomy, and a derived
set) with groundedness filters---content-word recall $\ge 0.5$ and
numeric binding---yielding $\sim$470{,}000 training decisions; we use
it to probe the data-scaling question.

\subsection{Public benchmarks}
GPQA main (448 graduate-level questions) and diamond subset (198) as
choice; SciFact dev (340 claim--abstract pairs) as noul and 3-way score;
plus general classification (SST-2, AG News, Enron spam, Banking77) to
probe out-of-domain transfer. Benchmark examples are not used for gradient
updates or temperature fitting; temperatures are fitted on the battery
dev split. Benchmark outcomes did inform the choice of release candidate.

\subsection{Candidate protocols and comparison limits}
The frozen release heads (\texttt{c\_*}) use learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$,
accumulation 1, warmup 200, and 2{,}000/3{,}000/3{,}000 example steps for
choice/noul/score. Heads for the adapted candidates (\texttt{dag\_*},
\texttt{da\_*}) and the small-backbone control (\texttt{eb\_*}) instead use
learning rate $10^{-3}$, accumulation 8, warmup 100, and 3{,}000 steps per
type. All use seed 7331, the same elite training files, the same head
architecture per type, and an ordinal auxiliary weight of 1 for score.
Thus these are candidate comparisons, not a controlled DAPT-only ablation.

DAPT uses LoRA rank 16, alpha 32, dropout 0.05, and the masked-token
objective implemented in \texttt{sciev.dapt}. With batch size 16
and sequence cap 1{,}024, the 2{,}000/5{,}000-step adapters correspond to
at most 32.8/81.9 million input token slots, not measured counts of unique
non-padding tokens. The 5{,}000-step run warm-starts from the
2{,}000-step adapter with a fresh optimizer and a schedule recomputed for
the shorter total budget; optimizer and RNG state were not restored.
The 1.4B \texttt{bw1\_sr} control is checkpoint-g20 from a 20-step run,
with a 768-token window versus 2{,}048 for LLaDA. It is an early-training
control, not evidence about the capacity of a fully trained 1.4B model.

\subsection{Backbone-only GPQA diagnostics}
\label{sec:backbone-diag}
Low GPQA accuracy could reflect either a weak decision readout or missing
backbone knowledge. We scored the cleaned GPQA cohort with the frozen
backbone alone, bypassing every trained head: three pseudo-log-likelihood
variants over the option text (full-mask, true leave-one-out, and
single-token label scoring) and two greedy masked-diffusion generation
modes (direct 6-token answer and 64-token reasoning).
Table~\ref{tab:backbone-diag} shows that no head-free protocol exceeds
chance by much: the best backbone-only readout (label scoring,
0.284/0.301) sits at or below the trained head (0.295/0.307 in the
matched systemone-v2 study), and 64-token reasoning generation returns
to chance. A dedicated choice head trained on our held-out
general-MCQA battery likewise leaves GPQA at 0.27 while reaching 0.84
on its own evaluation split. The consistent picture is that the
backbone, not the readout, limits GPQA: the knowledge required by
graduate-level, retrieval-resistant questions is not present in
extractable form. We therefore treat GPQA accuracy as a backbone
capability ceiling rather than a decision-layer defect, and the
remaining lever is grounding — supplying evidence in the state — not
further head optimization.

\subsection{Contrastive bi-encoder control}
\label{sec:biencoder}
To test whether the joint cross-encoding is itself the active
ingredient, we trained a CLM-style contrastive arm on the same frozen
backbone and the same training cohort: state/action projection heads
over mean-pooled backbone vectors, bidirectional InfoNCE with
same-question and in-batch negatives, protocol-identical held-out
temperature and acceptance policy. At our data scale the dot product
is far behind (Table~\ref{tab:biencoder}): 55--65 points down on the
in-domain suites and at chance on GPQA. The finding is consistent with
CLM-8B's own recipe, which relies on roughly $10^7$--$10^8$ contrastive
pairs plus synthetic hard negatives; at $\sim$5$\times 10^4$ decisions,
an option that never attends to the state cannot recover the
interaction. The arm is a single configuration, so we claim a
matched-scale comparison rather than a verdict on bi-encoders in
general. A natural follow-up use --- reusing the cached embeddings as
a cheap dot-product shortlist over decisions with more than four
options --- is also ruled out at this scale: on the 3080-row,
77-option banking77 suite the gold label reaches the top-4 of the
arm's ranking only 8.9\% of the time (recall@32: 46.5\%), and
suite-level action retrieval stays equally weak (recall@64
15--40\%). Any K$>$4 path therefore runs through the joint head
itself, e.g.\ option-chunked tournaments, not embedding prefiltering.

\subsection{Grounded GPQA: evidence rescues the head}
\label{sec:grounded-gpqa}
The backbone ceiling of Table~\ref{tab:backbone-diag} characterizes
\emph{closed-book} GPQA. The system's actual contract, however, is
grounded decisions: evidence in the state, typed options. To measure
whether the joint readout can exploit evidence at all, we built paired
grounded variants of the cleaned GPQA cohort --- identical options and
ordering, only the state gains an evidence block --- in two regimes:
\emph{oracle} appends the benchmark's expert-written explanation (a
gold-revealing upper bound), and \emph{nev} appends a non-expert
validator's own reasoning trace (noisy; validators average roughly a
third correct).

Table~\ref{tab:grounded-gpqa} separates the failure modes cleanly. On
GPQA-main, oracle evidence lifts the release head from 0.295 to 0.695
and the qa-v2 head from 0.273 to 0.707: given the right document, the
same backbone and head that sit at chance decide correctly on
graduate-level items --- and both heads converge, so evidence use
lives in the joint architecture, not the fine-tune. The noisy nev
trace recovers almost nothing (0.318 vs.\ 0.295), so the oracle--nev
gap prices retrieval quality: usable evidence must be
explanation-grade, not topical. A real production retrieval stack
confirms the price: BM25 top-3 passages from a 30.8M-article PubMed
index land at 0.304 on main for the release head---below nev and
statistically indistinguishable from closed-book (0.295)---and 0.292
for qa-v2. Coverage is not the bottleneck (only 2/440 questions found
no hit); the retrieved abstracts are topically adjacent rather than
explanation-grade, and GPQA is by construction resistant to
topical evidence. Closing the oracle gap therefore requires
retrieval that returns reasoning-bearing text---or a corpus that
covers physics and chemistry, which PubMed largely does not.
GPQA-diamond resists even the oracle
(0.326 vs.\ 0.307 baseline): on the hardest subset the residual
failure is option discrimination, not missing knowledge. Grounding
thus converts most of the closed-book ceiling, and what remains on
diamond is a harder interaction limit for this backbone.

The counterfactual-trained \texttt{alp25x} choice head
(Section~\ref{sec:antileak}) is the first arm to exceed the leaky
release on explanation-grade evidence (0.725 versus 0.695 on main
oracle) while simultaneously dropping 3--5 points on the noisy nev
and retrieved arms---the signature of a head that follows evidence
when it answers the question and commits less when it does not.
Anti-leakage training therefore did not merely remove the shortcut;
it improved the evidence-conditioned axis the benchmark actually
prices. The gain is attributable to the counterfactual labels, not
to abstention alone: the uniform-only variants \texttt{alpm1} and
\texttt{alp25p} score 0.682 and 0.686 on the same main-oracle arm,
below both the release head and \texttt{alp25x}.

($\pm$0.11) shows this gap fluctuates across runs. The matched design
therefore rejects a uniform verdict in either direction: adaptation
effects are task-dependent, not monotone, and the verification gain is
the most robust difference observed.

\subsection{Evidence controls expose option-side leakage}
High accuracy alone does not prove that decisions condition on the
supplied evidence. We therefore evaluate \emph{evidence controls}:
empty (the evidence span removed) and shuffle (the span replaced by a
donor passage from a different source group in the same split).
Controls keep the original question and proposed answer and are scored
for \emph{agreement with the reference answer}---not relabelled, since
altered evidence has no valid ground truth. Agreement far above chance
under destroyed evidence means the model decides without reading the
evidence, whether through parametric recall or option-side cues.

The controls qualify the headline accuracies. Choice agreement under
empty evidence is 0.87 (frozen): the task is largely solvable without
the passage, because gold answers are teacher-written text that matches
question phrasing while distractors are numeric perturbations or
topically foreign answers. Noul falls to its majority prior under
shuffled evidence, as it should. Score behaves as designed---agreement
collapses toward chance under wrong evidence---and is the only task
whose accuracy directly reflects evidence use. Both arms rely on
option-side leakage similarly; adaptation does not fix it. The matched
elite numbers should therefore be read as upper bounds on
evidence-grounded accuracy, and the battery needs distractors that are
indistinguishable without the passage before choice accuracy can be
claimed as grounded.

\paragraph{Stricter distractors reduce but do not remove leakage.}
Rebuilding the choice battery with a strict pool (\texttt{--hard-choice}:
same-passage swaps and numeric perturbations only, no cross-passage
fill; 733 records honestly excluded as untestable, $n=303$ eval)
changes accuracy to 0.914 (fr) / 0.868 (da) and empty-evidence
agreement to 0.795 / 0.766. The residual shows that same-passage
swaps---answers to \emph{other} questions---remain separable by
question--answer coherence alone; fully grounded choice requires
confounders to the \emph{same} question. Under shuffled evidence the
adapted arm agrees less than the frozen arm (0.722 versus 0.825),
consistent with better evidence filtering, matching its SciFact gain.

\subsection{Anti-leakage retraining removes the shortcut}
\label{sec:antileak}

The residual leakage could reflect either a capability limit of the
frozen backbone or a missing supervision signal. To separate the two we
rebuild the noul and score training sets with
\emph{evidence-corruption} counterparts: for each clean example we emit
an empty-evidence and a shuffled-evidence row whose label is forced to
the rejection class (\emph{false} for noul, level~0 for score)---the
same supervision the controls measure---subsampled to a fraction
$\rho$ of the clean count. Nothing else changes: same head, objective,
schedule, and eval files.

Three findings follow. First, the shortcut is a data artifact, not a
backbone limit: for noul, corrupted rows collapse every control to the
majority prior---balanced accuracy exactly 0.500---at every tested
$\rho$, including $\rho{=}0.0625$ where corrupted rows are only 14\%
of the mix. Score needs a larger dose: controls sit at the prior at
$\rho{\geq}0.25$, but at $\rho{=}0.0625$ shuffled-evidence agreement
leaks back to 0.386 (prior 0.334). A modest dose of the right
supervision suffices; a token one does not.

Second, the fix \emph{transfers}: on canonical SciFact noul, where the
answer must be read off the passage, the retrained heads improve over
the frozen release by 23--24 points (0.464$\to$0.693--0.702), while
the leaky head sits below its own majority prior. A head trained to
exploit option-side leakage cannot use evidence it is given; the
retrained heads can, and on the score proxy the $\rho{=}0.25$ head
matches the leaky release exactly (0.757).

Third, $\rho{=}0.25$ is the operating point, not $\rho{=}1$. Forcing
all corrupted rows to the rejection class shifts the training prior to
89\% \emph{false} (noul) and 78\% level-0 (score), against eval priors
of 67\% and 34\%, and the 14-point clean-accuracy drop on score at
$\rho{=}1$ is mostly that threshold shift. At $\rho{=}0.25$ score
recovers past the release (0.911 versus 0.896 clean) while holding
exact-prior controls; at $\rho{=}0.0625$ it still matches clean but
starts leaking again. Note also that the clean-eval accuracies of the
leaky arms are themselves inflated by option-side cues shared with the
training distribution, so the $\rho{=}0.25$ heads are the honest
release candidates even where the leaky head retains a nominal edge
(noul: 0.907 versus 0.931).

\paragraph{Choice needs a different supervision.}
The choice head has no rejection class---every row must select one of
the candidate answers---so corrupted-evidence rows cannot reuse the
label remap. We instead supervise the \emph{distribution}: corrupted
rows carry a uniform soft target and are trained with the KL term
alone (\texttt{--soft-weight 1.0}), expressing ``no usable evidence,
no preference'' without touching the label space. A second mechanism
targets evidence-following rather than abstention: provenance-tagged
\emph{counterfactual} rows keep question and options but minimally
edit the passage so that a documented distractor becomes the supported
claim (single-token \texttt{number\_perturb}-style edits located in
the evidence span), with the anti-leakage noul head arbitrating each
candidate as a \emph{Proposed answer} verifier
($\tau{=}0.6$, margin $0.2$). Two earlier donor schemes failed for
instructive reasons: topical near-passage transplants were rejected by
the teacher on all 230 candidates (max $p_\mathrm{true}{=}0.089$), and
\texttt{cross\_passage} provenance splices were likewise all-uniform
on 548 candidates---the question does not transfer to a foreign
passage, so honest supervision there is exactly the uniform target.

The counterfactual mechanism works where the naive transplant fails:
editing the passage rather than replacing it keeps the question
applicable, and the teacher confirms the provenance-intended
distractor in 50.5\% of editable cases (256 of 507), producing hard
labels whose gold distribution is position-balanced. The residual gap
between \texttt{alp25p} and \texttt{alp25x} on the controls is the
price of commitment: a head trained to follow edited evidence commits
more often under destroyed evidence (0.40--0.45 versus
0.26--0.32)---still less than half the release leakage---while
recovering clean accuracy. Which trade-off to ship depends on whether
the deployment contract prioritizes maximal abstention
(\texttt{alp25p}) or evidence sensitivity (\texttt{alp25x}); the
grounded-GPQA probe in Table~\ref{tab:grounded-gpqa} measures the
latter directly.

A caveat on the SciFact cells: Table~\ref{tab:public} was produced on
the legacy conversion ($n{=}340$), whose options are bare
single-token \emph{true}/\emph{false} candidates scored against
varying per-row prompt tails. The canonical \texttt{systemone-v2}
conversion scores full-phrase options (the verdict token followed by a
verbalized verdict sentence) under a fixed question template---the
same format the trained heads see in production. On the same claims,
the frozen head scores 0.850 in the legacy format but 0.464 under the
canonical encoding: the headline number was therefore sensitive to a
format the deployed model does not use. For reference, the third-party
JEV evaluation scored 0.888 on the same legacy files, so the
legacy-format comparison also holds externally. We report both rather
than choose; the anti-leakage comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:antileak}
are always same-file comparisons.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What a decision layer buys.}
A decision layer exposes probabilities without text generation or output
parsing. On the elite battery, the frozen heads have ECE 0.0253--0.1221
and choice automation@5\% of 0.8070. This retrospective operating curve
can inform routing to a slower component, but a deployment threshold
must be validated independently under its own data distribution.

\paragraph{Backbone and head must be evaluated together.}
Readout architecture changes accuracy even with a fixed backbone, while
candidate performance also varies with the backbone, context window,
and training schedule. The early bw1\_sr checkpoint cannot establish a
size-dependent knowledge ceiling. The backbone-only diagnostics of
Table~\ref{tab:backbone-diag} close the loop on GPQA: no head-free
scoring or generation protocol beats the trained head, so the residual
gap is backbone knowledge, not readout capacity. Likewise, proximity to
a published GPQA score under another inference protocol would not prove
that our head extracts all available knowledge. Grounding closes much
of the loop in the other direction (Table~\ref{tab:grounded-gpqa}):
with oracle evidence the same model reaches 0.70 on GPQA-main, so the
readout is not wasting latent competence --- but the noisy and
diamond cells bound how much a real retriever can buy back.

\paragraph{Candidate selection is not a causal adaptation study.}
Table~\ref{tab:candidates} supports retaining the frozen heads for this
release: the adapted candidates do not satisfy the transfer gate despite
some improved elite metrics. It does not establish that domain adaptation
necessarily sacrifices generalization, nor that a domain-native backbone
would avoid that trade-off. The matched comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:matched} holds head optimization fixed on
\texttt{systemone-v2} and shows the causal effect is task-dependent:
adaptation transfers to external verification while degrading ordinal
scoring. A fuller study should still repeat seeds, restore optimizer
state across interruptions, and reserve untouched evaluation data after
selection.

\paragraph{Relation to the ecosystem.}
Concurrent System-One implementations motivate typed decision interfaces.
CLM-8B \cite{clm} serves the same typed-question contract from a frozen
causal encoder whose state/action projection heads are trained
contrastively over large state--action corpora, while our implementation
explores a masked-diffusion backbone and deterministic option ordering;
we additionally train a CLM-style contrastive bi-encoder on our own
backbone and data as a matched-architecture control. Backbone diversity
also widens the ecosystem's reach: the released CLM-8B heads are tied to
the Qwen3-8B encoder, while this work serves the same typed-question
contract on a non-Qwen backbone for environments where that dependency
is excluded by model-approval or provenance policy. A numerical ranking
against other released systems would require matched dataset
conversions,
splits, and calibration protocols; the empirical tables here report only
our own evaluated candidates.

\section{Limitations}
Backbone pretraining may include benchmark-adjacent text. Heads are trained
on one decision distribution and on at most four options; Banking77
performance is poor. Score remains weaker than choice on the elite
battery. Results are single runs, without multi-seed uncertainty
estimates. Candidate comparisons differ in optimization, and the small
backbone is only an early-training control. External benchmarks informed
release selection, so they are not an untouched final test set. We report
archived evaluation outputs and their hashes, not a new evaluation of the
full backbone during release packaging.
